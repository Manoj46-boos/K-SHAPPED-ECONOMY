   107→## SECTION 4 — What "K-Shaped" Actually Means (7:50–10:45)
   108→
   109→**VO:** So: markets near record highs. Sentiment that crashed and only partly recovered. Same economy, same months. Economists have a name for this, and it's worth actually defining instead of just repeating.
   110→
   111→**[ON-SCREEN: simple animated "K" — one arm rising, one arm falling from the same point]**
   112→
   113→Economists at the Richmond Fed define it plainly: the economy is K-shaped when outcomes for the bottom of the income distribution get worse at the same time outcomes for the top get better. Same starting point. Same economy. Two lines that diverge — like the two strokes of the letter K.
   114→
   115→This isn't an internet theory. The New York Fed hosted an entire academic symposium on the K-shaped economy this spring. Economists at U.S. Bank, the Bank of America Institute, Goldman Sachs, and Moody's have all published research using this exact framework in 2026.
   116→
   117→**[ON-SCREEN: NY Fed retail spending chart by income bracket — small source tag: "NY Fed, Liberty Street Economics"]**
   118→
   119→And it shows up in hard data, not just vibes. New York Fed researchers tracked real retail spending since January 2023 and found it's been driven almost entirely by households earning more than $125,000 a year. Spending among households earning under $40,000 actually *fell* for a stretch — it didn't climb back to its January 2023 level until the middle of 2024.
   120→
   121→**[ON-SCREEN: gas pump graphic with two numbers — small source tag: "NY Fed, Liberty Street Economics"]**
   122→
   123→Remember that Iran-linked gas price spike from a minute ago — the one that tanked consumer sentiment? Split it by income and you can see the K in miniature. When energy prices spiked in March, higher-income households increased what they spent on gas by 19% — while barely changing how much they actually drove. Lower-income households cut their actual driving by 7%, and their gas bill still went up. Same price shock, at the same time, in the same country. Completely different lived experience.
   124→
   125→**[ON-SCREEN: recession-odds gauge swinging toward 48%, or simple bold text: "48%"]**
   126→
   127→Not every economist agrees on how severe this is, by the way — and that disagreement is itself useful. Goldman Sachs' chief U.S. economist initially argued the K-shape narrative was somewhat overstated. Moody's Mark Zandi has been far more alarmed, saying for much of the past year that roughly half of U.S. states were effectively in a state-level recession, with lower-income consumers, in his words, "hanging on by their fingertips." At his most alarmed point, in that same spring, as the Iran conflict escalated, Zandi's own recession model briefly hit 48% — practically a coin flip. Reasonable, serious people are reading the same data differently, which tells you this is genuinely contested, not a settled slam dunk in either direction.

   149→## SECTION 6 — What's Driving the "Down" Leg (12:36–15:48)
   150→
   151→**VO:** Now flip to the bottom of the K — and start with debt, because the numbers here are stark.
   152→
   153→**[ON-SCREEN: credit card delinquency chart, climbing — small source tag: "NY Fed"]**
   154→
   155→New York Fed data shows 13.1% of credit card balances — roughly one in eight — are at least 90 days past due, the highest that figure's been since 2011. Auto loan delinquencies just hit 5.6%, a record in the data. Total household debt sits at an all-time high of $18.8 trillion.
   156→
   157→And even inside that debt pile, there's a K-shape of its own: borrowers with strong credit — prime and super-prime — are largely fine. The delinquencies are concentrated in, and rising fastest among, the borrowers who had the least room to begin with: near-prime and subprime.
   158→
   159→**[ON-SCREEN: "core white-collar employment" chart, sector index down since 2023]**
   160→
   161→Here's a genuinely surprising wrinkle, too: for most of the last few decades, the office job was the "safe" one. That's flipped, at least at the margins. Employment in finance, information, and professional business services — call it core white-collar work — has shrunk about 2% since April 2023, while every other sector combined has grown almost 4% over the same span. University of Michigan data shows job-security confidence among the highest-earning third of Americans sitting at its lowest levels since the 2008–2009 financial crisis. It's not that blue-collar workers are suddenly thriving across the board — degree-holders still have meaningfully lower unemployment on average — but for the first time in a long time, the *direction of travel* on hiring anxiety has shifted toward the desk job, not away from it.
   162→
   163→**[ON-SCREEN: "who owns the stock market" pie chart — top 10% vs. everyone else — small source tag: "Federal Reserve"]**
   164→
   165→So who actually owns the stocks that are going up? Here's where it gets stark. Federal Reserve data shows the wealthiest one in ten U.S. households own around 87% of all stock market wealth, held directly and through retirement accounts. And that exposure is itself historic: stocks now make up roughly a third of total U.S. household wealth — a bigger share than during the 2021 meme-stock boom, bigger than at the peak of the dot-com bubble in 2000. Meanwhile, just over half of American adults — 58%, down from 62% a year earlier — own any stock at all right now, the first meaningful drop in stock ownership since 2016.
   166→
   167→Put that all together, and the "paradox" in the headline stops being a paradox. The market can be at record highs and genuinely be leaving most of the country behind — because most of the country was never really *in* the market to begin with, and the people who are in it have more riding on it than ever.
